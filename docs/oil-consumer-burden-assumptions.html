Oil Consumer Burden Model Assumptions

This note documents the assumptions behind the first increment of the oil-price scenario model. The model estimates how alternative crude oil prices change consumer fuel-spending burden by CEX income quintile.

Model output: outputs/oil_consumer_burden_scenarios.csv Primary metric: fuel spending share of total expenditure Scenario unit: dollars per barrel of WTI crude oil

Core Assumptions

  1. Oil-price scenarios are WTI crude oil prices. Scenario inputs are interpreted as dollars per barrel of West Texas Intermediate crude oil, not Brent, imported refiners' acquisition cost, diesel, jet fuel, or a broad energy index.
  2. Gasoline is the consumer transmission channel. The first model focuses on household fuel burden through retail regular gasoline prices. It does not separately model heating oil, natural gas, electricity, public transit fares, airfares, or freight pass-through.
  3. Oil-to-gasoline pass-through is linear. Annual average WTI prices and annual average retail regular gasoline prices are fit with a simple linear relationship. The same slope and intercept are used for all scenarios.
  4. The pass-through relationship is estimated from historical annual averages. The model aggregates FRED oil and gasoline observations to calendar-year averages before fitting pass-through. This intentionally smooths weekly and monthly volatility.
  5. Consumer gasoline quantity is held constant. Baseline gasoline-and-other-fuels spending is scaled by the ratio of scenario gasoline price to baseline gasoline price. Consumers do not drive less, substitute vehicles, switch fuels, or change location in response to higher prices.
  6. CEX gasoline-and-other-fuels spending is the exposure base. Fuel burden uses the CEX item Gasoline and other fuels for each income quintile. The item is a consumer expenditure measure, not a physical gallons-consumed measure.
  7. Total annual expenditures are the burden denominator. Burden is calculated as scenario fuel spending divided by total annual CEX expenditures for the same income quintile. The denominator is not after-tax income, disposable income, wealth, or official poverty resources.
  8. The baseline is the latest common year available across required datasets. The model chooses the most recent year with CEX fuel spending, CEX total expenditures, and FRED annual gasoline prices. Current local CEX data are expected to make this 2024 once the oil/gasoline fetch succeeds.
  9. Income quintiles are treated as stable groups. The model compares burden by reported CEX income quintile and does not model households moving between quintiles as prices, wages, or employment conditions change.
  10. All values are nominal. The output is a nominal-dollar scenario exercise. It does not deflate spending, rebase to real dollars, or estimate purchasing-power loss after general inflation.
  11. There are no macro feedback effects. The model does not estimate effects on GDP, employment, wages, interest rates, inflation expectations, government transfers, or broader PCE categories.
  12. Scenarios are comparative statics. Each oil price is evaluated as a standalone steady scenario against the same baseline. The model does not simulate a path over time, adjustment lags, inventories, refinery outages, taxes, or regional price dispersion.

Model Mechanics

Step Assumption embedded in the step
Fetch price inputs FRED WTI crude and U.S. regular gasoline series are adequate national price anchors.
Average by year Annual averages are the right time scale for matching annual CEX spending.
Fit pass-through A single historical linear relationship can translate scenario oil prices into gasoline prices.
Select baseline The latest common year is the best current snapshot for household exposure.
Scale fuel spending Fuel quantities are unchanged, so spending moves proportionally with gasoline prices.
Compute burden Total CEX expenditure is the appropriate first-pass measure of the household budget base.

Supply Shock Mode

The model can estimate crude oil prices from net unresolved supply gaps before running the existing consumer burden calculation. This mode treats the shock as the remaining crude supply shortfall after spare capacity, inventory draws, and policy responses have already been considered.

  1. Supply shocks are net gaps. A shock of 5 means the market is short 5 million barrels per day after offsets, not that gross production fell by 5 million barrels per day before any response.
  2. The default shock set is a stress set. If supply-shock mode is used without explicit shock values, the model evaluates 1, 3, 5, 8, 10 million barrels per day.
  3. Baseline supply defaults to 100 million barrels per day. This is a documented round assumption, not a fetched live estimate. It can be changed with --baseline-supply-mbd.
  4. Crude demand elasticity defaults to -0.10. This short-run elasticity can be tuned with --crude-demand-elasticity. More negative values produce smaller price moves for the same supply gap; values closer to zero produce larger price moves.
  5. Baseline oil price comes from processed FRED data unless overridden. The model uses the latest annual WTI value available in the processed oil/gasoline price data, unless --baseline-oil-price is supplied.
Formula Meaning
supply_gap_share = shock_mbd / baseline_supply_mbd Converts the unresolved supply gap into a percent of baseline crude supply.
price_change_share = supply_gap_share / abs(elasticity) Uses short-run demand elasticity to estimate the crude price response.
scenario_oil_price = baseline_oil_price * (1 + price_change_share) Creates the WTI scenario that feeds into gasoline pass-through and consumer burden.

Known Limits

  1. The model is useful for burden sensitivity, not for forecasting retail gasoline prices.
  2. It likely overstates burden at very high oil prices because real consumers reduce usage over time.
  3. It may understate broader cost pressure because it excludes indirect fuel costs embedded in other goods and services.
  4. It does not distinguish rural, suburban, and urban households, which likely have different gasoline exposure.
  5. It does not account for state gasoline taxes, refinery margins, seasonal blends, or regional supply constraints.
  6. It uses aggregate income-quintile spending, so it does not show within-quintile variation.

Assumptions To Revisit Later

The first assumptions to relax should be the constant-quantity assumption and the single national gasoline-price assumption. Those are the two places where the model is most likely to be directionally right but mechanically too simple.
  1. Add a gasoline demand elasticity so high-price scenarios reduce implied consumption.
  2. Add regional CEX or state gasoline-price detail if the analysis needs geographic exposure.
  3. Compare burden against after-tax income as a second metric, not a replacement for expenditure share.
  4. Add indirect energy exposure through food, transportation services, and goods delivery.
  5. Estimate separate pass-through relationships for rising versus falling oil-price regimes.
